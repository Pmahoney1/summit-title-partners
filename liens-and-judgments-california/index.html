Selling a California House With Liens or Judgments

A lawsuit you lost years ago. A contractor who says he was never paid. An HOA balance, an IRS notice, a city abatement bill, back child support. How liens and judgments work in California

How a court judgment becomes a lien on your house

A judgment by itself does not touch your house. The creditor has to record an abstract of judgment with the county recorder, and that recording creates a judgment lien on real property (Code of Civil Procedure section 697.310). The lien attaches to every interest you own in that county, and to any property you acquire there later (section 697.340). It does not reach property in a county where no abstract was recorded. That is why a lawsuit over a credit card or a business ends up sitting on the house.

How long a judgment lien lasts

A money judgment in California is enforceable for 10 years from the date it was entered (section 683.020), and the judgment lien on real property lasts for the same 10 years (section 697.310). The creditor can renew the judgment by filing an application with the court, which extends it for another 10 years from the filing date (section 683.120). For the lien on your house to carry forward, the creditor must also record a certified copy of the renewal application with the county recorder before the old lien runs out (section 683.180). Some consumer-debt and medical-debt judgments can now be renewed only once, for five years (sections 683.110 and 683.120).

Without a timely renewal, the judgment can no longer be enforced and its liens are extinguished, though the abstract still sits in the county records.

The homestead exemption, and what it does in a sale

California's homestead exemption protects equity in the home you live in from judgment creditors. The statute sets the amount at no less than $300,000 and no more than $600,000 depending on the county, and both figures have been adjusted for inflation every year since January 1, 2022, so the current amounts are higher (section 704.730).

What the exemption does depends on the paperwork. If you recorded a homestead declaration before the creditor recorded its abstract, the judgment lien attaches only to equity above your existing loans plus the exemption amount (section 704.950). Without a recorded declaration, the automatic homestead mainly protects you if a creditor goes to court to force a sale. In a voluntary sale, a recorded judgment lien generally has to be paid, negotiated, or otherwise resolved before title can transfer. How much protection applies to your sale is a question we take to an attorney, because the answer can be worth a great deal. A declaration does not block support judgments.

Who gets paid first: lien priority

The general rule is first in time, first in right: liens on the same property rank by the date they were created (Civil Code section 2897). For most liens, that is the recording date. The big exception is property taxes. Property tax liens have priority over every other lien on the property, regardless of when the other liens were created (Revenue and Taxation Code section 2192.1). Delinquent taxes are always paid first at closing; see Behind on Property Taxes.

When there is not enough to go around, a lien near the bottom of the stack is often the one most willing to negotiate.

Mechanic's liens

A contractor, subcontractor, or supplier who was not paid for work on the property can record a mechanic's lien. A direct contractor has to record within 90 days after the work is completed, or within 60 days after the owner records a notice of completion, whichever comes first (Civil Code section 8412). After recording, the claimant must file suit to enforce the lien within 90 days, or the lien expires and becomes unenforceable (section 8460).

A lien past that mark still shows on title, and the owner can petition the court for an order releasing the property (section 8480). If a live lien is disputed, a lien release bond for 125 percent of the claim moves it off the house (section 8424), so a sale can close while the dispute continues.

HOA assessment liens

Assessments become delinquent 15 days after they are due, and the association can add a late charge and interest of up to 12 percent a year (Civil Code section 5650). The lien is created by recording a notice of delinquent assessment with the county recorder (section 5675).

Many owners do not know this: the association cannot use the lien to force a sale of the home for delinquent assessments under $1,800, not counting late charges, fees, interest, and collection costs, unless the assessments are more than 12 months delinquent (section 5720).

IRS and California tax liens

A federal tax lien arises when an assessed tax goes unpaid after the IRS demands it, and it covers all your property, including the house. The IRS releases it within 30 days after the debt is paid. If a sale will not pay the full balance, you can apply for a certificate of discharge, which removes the lien from the house being sold, on Form 14135 (Internal Revenue Code section 6325(b), explained in IRS Publication 783). The IRS asks for the application at least 45 days before the closing date.

California's tax agencies, including the Franchise Tax Board and the Employment Development Department, record a notice of state tax lien with the county recorder (Government Code section 7171). A state tax lien lasts 10 years and can be extended for additional 10-year periods by recording a new notice (section 7172). In a sale, escrow requests a payoff demand and the lien is released once paid. When proceeds fall short, the Franchise Tax Board may grant a partial release or subordination.

Child support liens

Unpaid child, family, or spousal support becomes a lien on real property when an abstract, a notice of support judgment, or a certified copy of the judgment is recorded (Code of Civil Procedure section 697.320). Unlike an ordinary judgment, a support judgment is enforceable until it is paid in full and does not need to be renewed (Family Code section 291). These liens have to be paid or released.

Code enforcement and nuisance abatement liens

When a city or county abates a nuisance on a property, it can recover the cost from the owner. A city can record a nuisance abatement lien, which has the force, effect, and priority of a judgment lien from the date it is recorded (Government Code section 38773.1). A county can also add the cost to the property tax bill as a special assessment, collected the same way as property taxes, with the same penalties and the same tax-sale process (section 25845). That route is easy to miss, because it looks like a bigger tax bill.

Old liens that still show up on title

County records do not clean themselves. Expired judgments, unenforced mechanic's liens, and deeds of trust paid off decades ago but never reconveyed all still appear in a title search. Under California law, an old mortgage or deed of trust generally expires 10 years after its final maturity date, or 60 years after it was recorded if no maturity date appears (Civil Code section 882.020). Even so, a title insurer may require a recorded release or court order before insuring a sale. Our Title Problems page covers how those get cleared.

Your realistic options

Which of these are open depends on what is owed, to whom, in what order, and whether any of it has expired. The investigation establishes that. Several of these do not involve us at all.

Keep the house and pay off or cure the liens

Paying a lien in full gets a recorded release. Some liens can be cured without paying at all: a mechanic's lien recorded late or never enforced, or a judgment that has quietly expired.

Payment plans or a negotiated payoff

Many lienholders will take a payment plan, and some will accept less in exchange for being paid now, especially holders of old judgments and junior liens. The IRS and state agencies have their own installment programs. Any negotiated payoff should be in writing and require a recorded release.

Sell with time

If you need time to settle with a creditor, wait out an expiration date, or move, a sale can be structured with a longer close while releases and payoff demands come in.

Sell as-is, with the liens paid from escrow

The most common answer. Escrow orders payoff demands from every lienholder, pays them from the proceeds in priority order, collects the releases, and sends you what is left. You do not pay anything off before listing. A negotiated payoff letter goes to escrow the same way.

A creative structure or a direct purchase

When the liens exceed the house's value, a lienholder will not cooperate, or you need certainty on timing, a conventional listing may not fit. The alternatives, including a structured sale, a sale with time, or a direct purchase by Summit Title Partners, are on our Flexible Solutions page. We may earn from a transaction we join, and we say so before you choose. We will not propose one if a plain sale through escrow serves you better.

Doing nothing

Occasionally right, when an unrenewed judgment is close to its 10-year mark. Usually expensive. Judgments accrue interest, HOA balances grow, tax liens get extended, support liens never expire, and property taxes keep moving toward the county's power to sell.

Common questions

Can I sell my house in California if there is a judgment lien on it?

Yes. The lien has to be paid, negotiated, or released before title transfers, but in most sales escrow pays it from the proceeds at closing, so you do not need cash up front.

How long does a judgment lien last in California?

Ten years from the date the judgment was entered. The creditor can renew it for another 10 years by filing with the court and recording the renewal with the county recorder before the lien expires.

Does the homestead exemption protect me when I sell?

It depends on whether you recorded a homestead declaration before the judgment. With one, the lien attaches only to equity above your loans and the exemption. Without one, the exemption mainly protects against a court-ordered sale, and the lien generally has to be resolved in a voluntary sale. An attorney should confirm how it applies to yours.

Which liens get paid first when a house is sold?

Property taxes, always, regardless of when the other liens were recorded. After that, liens generally rank by the date they were created, so the earliest recorded lien is paid first.

There is an IRS lien on my house. Can I still sell?

Yes. If the sale covers the balance, escrow pays the IRS and the lien is released. If not, you can apply for a certificate of discharge on Form 14135, which removes the lien from the house. The IRS asks for the application at least 45 days before closing.

Can lienholders accept less than what they are owed?

Often, especially on old judgments and junior liens that would otherwise receive little. Get it in writing, with a recorded release required once paid. No creditor is obligated to agree.

A lien on my title is decades old. Do I still have to deal with it?

Possibly not, but you have to prove it. Unrenewed judgments expire after 10 years, unenforced mechanic's liens after 90 days, and old deeds of trust under Civil Code section 882.020. A title company will usually still want a recorded release or court order.
