How title problems work in California

What a cloud on title is

Title is the record of who owns a property and what claims are attached to it, kept at the county recorder's office as a chain of documents going back decades. A cloud is anything in that record, or missing from it, that raises doubt about who owns the property or whether it is free of a claim. A title company's preliminary report lists each one as an exception, and each has to be cleared, insured over, or accepted by the buyer before escrow can close.

Breaks in the chain of title

Every owner should have received title from the owner before them in an unbroken line of recorded documents. A break happens when a link is missing: a deed signed but never recorded, an owner who died with no transfer recorded, or a deed from someone who was never on title.

An unrecorded deed is still valid between the people who signed it (Civil Code section 1217), so a properly signed deed found in a drawer can often still be recorded. The risk is that it loses out to a later buyer or lender who paid value without knowing about it and recorded first (Civil Code section 1214).

Recording errors

Small mistakes stall a surprising number of sales: a wrong legal description, a name spelled differently from one document to the next, a missing middle initial that makes someone else's judgment look like yours, or a deed that was never properly notarized. A deed generally has to be acknowledged before the county recorder will accept it (Government Code section 27287).

Most of these are fixed with a corrective deed, signed by the original grantor and recorded to restate the transfer correctly. For a minor typo, some title companies will accept a recorded affidavit from whoever prepared the original document, sometimes called a scrivener's affidavit. When the original grantor has died or cannot be found, a court order may be needed instead.

Old loans that were paid off but never released

When a deed of trust is paid off, California requires the lender to send the trustee the release paperwork within 30 calendar days, and the trustee to record a full reconveyance within 21 calendar days after receiving it (Civil Code section 2941). It does not always happen, especially when the lender was sold, merged, or failed.

There is a backstop. If no reconveyance is recorded within 75 calendar days after payoff, a title insurance company may record a release of the obligation after mailing 10 days' notice to the trustee, the borrower, and the lender of record. A violator owes $500 plus actual damages. Clearing an old loan means proving it was paid, finding whoever holds it now, and getting a reconveyance or release recorded. A payoff statement or a later refinance closing statement makes this far easier.

Ancient mortgages and California's marketable record title rules

Sometimes a decades-old deed of trust has no payoff record and the lender is long gone. California's marketable record title statutes address this (Civil Code section 880.020 and following). Under Civil Code section 882.020, the lien expires 10 years after the final maturity date shown in the recorded documents, or 60 years after recording if no maturity date can be found, unless the lender recorded a notice of intent to preserve it in time. An expired lien can no longer be enforced, which often lets a title company remove it.

Old easements can expire too. Under Civil Code section 887.050, an easement is abandoned if for the 20 years before an action is brought it was not used, no separate property tax was paid on it, and no document creating or transferring it was recorded.

An owner on title who has died

If the owner held title in joint tenancy or as community property with right of survivorship, the survivor records an affidavit of death with a certified death certificate (Probate Code section 210). If the house was in a living trust, the successor trustee records an affidavit of death of trustee.

If the house was in the deceased owner's name alone, a court procedure is required. For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, a primary residence worth up to $750,000 can usually be transferred by a single petition under Probate Code section 13151. Real property worth $69,625 or less can pass by affidavit under Probate Code section 13200. A house meant for a trust but never deeded into it usually needs a petition under Probate Code section 850. Our Inherited Property page covers each route.

Missing heirs

When an owner died without a will and nothing was filed, every heir under intestate succession owns a share, and the heirs of any heir who has since died own theirs. A title company will not insure a sale until every interest is accounted for. That means building a family tree, finding people who moved away decades ago, and getting their signatures or a court order. This research is a large part of our work.

Forged deeds and wild deeds

Under long-standing California case law, a forged deed is void and passes no title, even to a later buyer who did not know. It still sits in the county record, though, and usually takes a court order to remove. A wild deed is a recorded deed from someone who was never in the chain of title, whether an honest mistake against the wrong parcel or something worse. Either way it is a competing claim that has to be explained or cleared.

Quiet title actions

When a cloud cannot be cleared by agreement or a recorded document, the remedy is usually a quiet title action, a lawsuit to establish title against adverse claims (Code of Civil Procedure section 760.010 and following). The complaint must be verified (section 761.020), and a notice of pending action is recorded immediately on filing (section 761.010).

Missing defendants can be served by publication only after the court is satisfied that reasonable diligence was used to find them (section 763.010), and the court cannot enter a default judgment. It must take evidence of the plaintiff's title even when nobody opposes it (section 764.010). Uncontested cases commonly take many months, contested ones longer. Quiet title needs a licensed attorney, and we coordinate with one when a case calls for it.

What title insurance does and does not fix

An owner's policy may cover a defect that existed before you bought and was not listed as an exception, such as a forged deed in the chain, so call the insurer first. But a policy pays covered losses or defends covered claims. It does not clean up the record by itself, and it does not cover problems that arose after you bought or the exceptions listed in the policy.

What curative work actually involves

The steps are nearly always the same. Pull the full chain of title. Identify each cloud and why it exists. Choose the simplest effective fix for each: an affidavit, a reconveyance, a corrective deed, signatures from heirs or prior owners, a probate petition, or a quiet title action. Gather the proof, such as payoff records, death certificates, and old escrow files. Get the documents signed, recorded, or filed. Then confirm with the title company that the exception is removed.

Your realistic options

Which of these are open depends on the cloud, who has to sign, and whether a court is involved. The investigation establishes that. Several of these do not involve us at all.

Keep the property and clear the title

Clearing the title still matters if you stay. It protects you when you refinance, leave the house to family, or eventually sell. Many clouds, like an unreleased loan or a missing affidavit of death, cost little more than recording fees once the right documents are found.

Use your title insurance or your old escrow

If the defect predates your purchase, file a claim on your owner's policy. If an old loan was paid through escrow, the title company that handled the payoff may be able to obtain the reconveyance or record a release. Neither involves us, and both are worth trying first.

Sell with time to clear the title

Many title problems can be cleared during a longer escrow. A buyer who understands the issue may agree to a close date that allows time for a reconveyance, corrective deed, or probate petition.

Sell with the problem still on the title

Some buyers will purchase with a known cloud and clear it themselves, usually at a lower price that reflects the time and risk. This can make sense when the fix is a lawsuit or the timeline matters more than the price.

A structured sale or a direct purchase

When the title needs curative work and you also need time or a way to deal with co-owners, a conventional listing may not fit. The alternatives, including a sale with a long close, a creative structure, or a direct purchase by Summit Title Partners, are on our Flexible Solutions page. We may earn from a transaction we join, and we say so before you choose. We will not propose one if clearing the title and selling through escrow serves you better.

Doing nothing

Also an option, and it tends to get more expensive. Lenders get absorbed into other banks, and heirs who could have signed die, leaving their children to sign. A problem that takes a recorded affidavit this year can take a court petition in five.

Common questions

What is a cloud on title?

Anything in the county's recorded documents, or missing from them, that creates doubt about who owns a property or whether it is free of a claim, such as an unreleased old loan, a deceased owner still on the deed, a recording error, or a missing heir.

I paid off my mortgage years ago, but it still shows on my title. What do I do?

The lender was required to have a reconveyance recorded (Civil Code section 2941). If it never was, the current holder of the loan can still provide one, and once 75 days have passed since payoff a title insurance company may record a release after giving 10 days' notice. Proof of payoff speeds this up.

How long does it take to clear a title problem in California?

It depends on the fix. A missing reconveyance or affidavit of death can take a few weeks. A probate petition usually takes a few months. A quiet title action commonly takes many months, longer if contested.

What is a quiet title action, and do I need one?

A lawsuit to establish title against adverse claims, under Code of Civil Procedure section 760.010 and following. You need one when a cloud cannot be cleared by agreement or a recorded document, such as a forged deed or a competing ownership claim. It requires an attorney.

Can I sell my house with a title problem?

Sometimes. Many problems are cleared during escrow. Some buyers will purchase with a known cloud, usually at a lower price. What the buyer's title company will insure decides it, and the investigation tells you that before you list.

My parent is still on the deed but has passed away. Can I sell?

Not until the title is updated. With joint tenancy or a trust, an affidavit of death is often enough. If the house was in your parent's name alone, many homes qualify for a single petition under Probate Code section 13151 rather than full probate.
