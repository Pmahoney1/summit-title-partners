Behind on Property Taxes in California? Your Options Before a Tax Sale

If you are behind on property taxes in California, you probably have the envelopes to prove it. First a delinquency notice. Then a notice that the property is tax-defaulted. Eventually a letter warning that the county can sell it. Each letter gets sharper, and none of them explain what you can actually do.

Here is the short version. California gives homeowners more time and more options than most people realize. Those options close in a fixed order, and the last one closes at the end of the business day before a tax sale. Where you stand in that order decides which options are still open.

How delinquent property taxes work in California

California property tax is billed once a year and paid in two installments. The timeline below is the same in every county. The dollar amounts, the redemption fee, and the county's cost charges vary, so the number to work from is always the redemption amount on the statement from your county tax collector.

The first year: delinquent

The first installment is due November 1 and becomes delinquent after December 10. The second is due February 1 and becomes delinquent after April 10. Each unpaid installment picks up a 10 percent penalty the day it goes delinquent, and the county adds a delinquent cost to the second installment (Revenue and Taxation Code sections 2617 and 2618). At this stage you are delinquent, not in default. The county cannot sell the property and no lien beyond the ordinary tax lien has been created.

July 1: tax-defaulted

If any part of the year's taxes is still unpaid at the end of the fiscal year on June 30, the property becomes tax-defaulted on July 1 (section 3436). This is the status you will see on the tax bill and in the recorder's index. From here, the unpaid amount accrues redemption penalties of 1.5 percent per month, which is 18 percent a year, plus a redemption fee (sections 4102 and 4103). Penalties keep compounding on the defaulted balance every month until it is paid.

Being tax-defaulted does not mean the county owns the property or is about to sell it. You still own it, you can still live in it, and you can still sell it. What has changed is that the clock to the next stage has started.

Years one through five: the redemption period

Paying off a tax-defaulted balance is called redemption. For residential property you can redeem at any time during the five years after the property became tax-defaulted, either by paying the full redemption amount or by opening an installment plan (sections 4101 and 4216). Nothing is sold during this window. In practice this is where most homeowners we talk to are, and most of them believe they have far less time than they do.

One mortgage-related warning belongs here. If there is a loan on the property, the servicer will usually see the default, advance the taxes to the county, and add the amount to what you owe. That protects the lender's lien, but it can also put the loan itself into default. If you have a mortgage, the tax problem and the loan need to be handled together.

After five years: the county's power to sell

Five years after the property became tax-defaulted, or three years for nonresidential commercial property, the property becomes subject to the tax collector's power to sell (section 3691). The county does not have to sell immediately, but it now can, and most counties schedule the property for the next public auction once the notices required by section 3701 have gone out. Property damaged in a declared disaster and not substantially repaired has this period tolled, so a home damaged in a wildfire or flood may have more time than the notice suggests.

The day before the sale: redemption ends

The right to redeem ends at the close of business on the last business day before the sale (section 3707). Up to that point you can pay the full redemption amount and stop the sale. The installment plan is no longer available once the property is subject to the power to sell, so in the final stretch the only way to keep the property is to pay in full. After the sale, the tax deed transfers the property to the buyer and the former owner's right to redeem is gone.

After a sale: excess proceeds

If the property sells for more than the taxes, penalties, and costs owed, the difference is held by the county as excess proceeds. Lienholders of record and then the former owner, in that order, can file a claim, and the claim must be received within one year of the date the tax deed is recorded (section 4675). This is the last money a homeowner can recover from a tax sale, and the county does not go looking for you to pay it. If you have already lost a property to a tax sale, we can check whether a claim is still open.

Your realistic options

Every option below is real, and the first three do not require Summit Title Partners at all. Which ones are still open depends on where your property is on the timeline above, which is the first thing our investigation establishes.

Pay the redemption amount in full

If the money exists, this is the cleanest answer. The redemption amount from the tax collector includes every year's taxes, penalties, and fees, and paying it clears the default the same day. Common sources are savings, a family member, or a refinance or equity line, though many lenders will not fund while the property is tax-defaulted, which is why the next two options exist.

Open an installment plan of redemption

California lets you pay off defaulted taxes over up to five years (sections 4216 through 4219). To open a plan you pay 20 percent or more of the redemption amount plus the processing fee, and then at least 20 percent of the balance each year by April 10, with interest on the unpaid balance. You must also keep each new year's taxes current while the plan is open. If you miss a payment the plan defaults and the full balance is due again, and a plan cannot be opened once the property has become subject to the power to sell. Counties administer plans through the tax collector's office, and the terms are on a one-page contract you sign there.

Apply for Property Tax Postponement

The State Controller's Property Tax Postponement Program pays the current year's property taxes for homeowners who are at least 62, or blind, or disabled, who live in the home as a principal residence, hold at least 40 percent equity, and have household income under a limit the Controller sets each year. The state records a lien and is repaid when the home is sold, refinanced, or transferred. Applications are accepted from October 1 through February 10 each year. It is designed around current taxes, so if you already have defaulted years it usually works alongside an installment plan rather than instead of one. Ask the Controller's office or your county tax collector how your defaulted years would be handled.

A tax relief loan

Private lenders will pay the county on your behalf and take a lien on the property, repaid over a set term. This stops the 18 percent redemption penalty and takes the property off the county's list. The trade is a new secured debt at a cost that varies by lender, and it only makes sense if the monthly payment is one you can actually carry. We do not lend money. If this is the right path, we will tell you, and we will tell you what to compare.

Sell with time to relocate

If keeping the property is not realistic, selling before the county does is almost always better than waiting. In an ordinary sale the defaulted taxes are paid out of your proceeds at closing and you keep the rest. At a tax sale the county recovers only what it is owed, the property goes to the highest bidder, and you have to file a claim to get what is left. A sale can be structured with a closing date and move-out date that give you time to find the next place, and we help with that structure.

A creative structure or a direct purchase

Some situations call for something other than a listing: a sale with a leaseback, a structured payoff with a partner, or a direct purchase by Summit Title Partners. These are on our Flexible Solutions page, including when each one fits and when it does not. Two things to know up front. We may earn from a transaction we join, and we say so before you decide. And we will not propose one if a simpler option that does not involve us would serve you better.

Doing nothing

This is also an option, so here is what it costs. The balance grows 18 percent a year. At the five-year mark the property becomes subject to sale. The county sells it, the tax deed records, and any equity above what the county was owed sits in an excess proceeds account waiting for a claim that lienholders get to file first. Of every path on this page, this is the only one that cannot be undone.

Common questions

How many years can you be behind on property taxes in California before the county can sell?

Five years from the date the property became tax-defaulted for residential property, and three years for nonresidential commercial property (Revenue and Taxation Code section 3691). The property becomes tax-defaulted on the July 1 after the first unpaid year, so the first sale date is usually a little more than five years after the first missed payment.

Can I stop a tax sale the week before?

Yes, by paying the full redemption amount before the close of business on the last business day before the sale (section 3707). An installment plan is not available at that point, because the property is already subject to the power to sell, so the only way to stop it is payment in full.

Can I still sell my house if the taxes are tax-defaulted?

Yes. A tax default does not stop a sale. The defaulted taxes, penalties, and fees are paid from your proceeds through escrow, and the buyer takes the property with the taxes cleared. The sale has to close before the right of redemption ends.

Does the county take my house when it becomes tax-defaulted?

No. Tax-defaulted is a status, not a transfer. You keep ownership and possession, and you can redeem at any time during the five-year period. The county only gains the power to sell after that period, and only sells after giving the notices the law requires.

The owner on the title died and the taxes are behind. What now?

This is one of the most common situations we investigate. The tax deadlines keep running after a death, and the county will sell a deceased owner's property on the same schedule. The heirs can usually redeem, open a plan, or sell, but the path depends on whether there is a trust, a will, a probate, or none of those. Our inherited property page covers the California rules, and the investigation sorts out which ones apply.

What is a tax relief loan, and is it a good idea?

A tax relief loan is a private loan that pays the county and takes a lien on the property. It stops the county's 18 percent redemption penalty and removes the property from the sale list. It is a good idea only when the payment is affordable and the property is worth keeping. It is a bad idea when it just moves the problem to a lender with its own default remedies. We do not lend, so our answer is not tied to the outcome.

Does the Property Tax Postponement Program cover back taxes?

The program postpones current-year taxes for eligible homeowners who are 62 or older, blind, or disabled, with at least 40 percent equity and income under the annual limit. It is built around current taxes, so defaulted prior years are typically handled with an installment plan or payment alongside it. The State Controller's office confirms eligibility and how prior years are treated.
